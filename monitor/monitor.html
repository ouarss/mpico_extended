Hardware

Touch sensing is done by three MPR121 capacitive controllers on the I2C bus, at addresses 0x5A, 0x5B and 0x5C. Each carries twelve electrodes, so there are 36 electrodes in total.

The playfield has 34 zones (A1–A8, B1–B8, C1–C2, D1–D8, E1–E8). Each zone is fed by one electrode; the electrode→zone assignment is the mapping (two electrodes are left unconnected, marked XX).

10x5B12–23
20x5C24–35

Electrode e lives on sensor floor(e/12), channel e mod 12.

How detection works

In one line: the sensor outputs a big raw number all the time; a finger makes that number drop; the firmware turns the drop into a delta that is 0 at rest and rises with the touch.

Step by step: each electrode constantly reports a raw reading (filtered), around 900 when nothing touches it - not 0. The firmware memorises that resting value: the baseline.

A finger near the pad steals a bit of charge, so the raw reading drops below the baseline (say 900 → 815).

The delta is simply « how far below rest am I right now »:

                    at rest      finger on the pad
filtered (raw)         900    ->     815     the reading DROPS
baseline (memorised)   900           900     the reference stays put
delta = 900 - 815        0            85     0 at rest, RISES when touched

So the reference is the baseline, and the delta never goes negative: at rest it sits at 0 (tiny noise aside), and a touch only makes it climb. If the raw reading ever drifts above the baseline, the delta is clamped to 0.

The decision is then a plain comparison on that delta:

delta
   85            .------.
                 |      |
   40 --------- ON ------------- threshold ON: zone fires at 40
   30 ----------|------ OFF ---- threshold OFF: 40 x (1 - 25% hyst) = 30
    0 __________'        '______
        rest     press    release

Tuning available

  1. Averaging - the last few frames are averaged (avg) to shed the couple of counts of noise a single frame carries. Example - readings 5, 6, 60, 5 (that 60 is a noise spike). With avg 1 the firmware sees 60 and may fire; with avg 4 it sees (5+6+60+5)/4 = 19: no trigger. The cost is real lag: one electrode is re-read every ~3 ms, so avg 4 spreads a press over ~12 ms and avg 10 over ~30 ms - clearly felt in play. Stay at 1-3.
  2. Threshold - a zone turns on when its delta reaches the zone's threshold. Higher threshold means less sensitive. Example - a zone rests at 5-10 and a firm press peaks at 120: a threshold of 40 clears the noise and still catches a quick tap. At 10 it fires on its own; at 110 quick taps are missed.
  3. Release margin (hyst) - once on, a zone stays on until the delta falls under threshold x (1 - hyst/100). This stops a finger resting at the limit from flickering on and off. Example - threshold 50, hyst 25%: the zone turns on at 50 and only releases below 37; anything wobbling between 37 and 50 changes nothing. A firm tap never needs it - it matters on holds and slides, where a light or lifting finger hovers near the threshold.
  4. Debounce - a change must hold for debounce on / debounce off frames before it is accepted, filtering out one-frame glitches. Example - with on 3, a spike lasting one reading never makes 3 in a row and is dropped; a finger, present for dozens of readings, passes. Each step costs ~3 ms (an electrode is re-read every 3rd frame).
  5. Gain (CDC / CDT) - the sensor's measuring drive: CDC is the current pushed into the electrode (uA), CDT how long it is pushed. This is not a filter to minimise - it powers the measurement itself: at 0 no charge is injected and nothing is measured at all. 16 / 1 sit mid-range and put a typical pad in the middle of the 0-1023 reading scale; auto-config then trims the current per electrode. Example - press peaks at 40 with noise around 8; double the gain and they read ~80 and ~16 - bigger numbers, same ratio. After any change, re-run the thresholds (every number just moved).
  6. Filter (FFI / SFI / ESI) - the chip's own smoothing, applied before the firmware ever reads the value: FFI and SFI average several samples, ESI sets the wait between samples. Example - 0 1 0 (default) is light and responsive; 0 1 3 spreads the samples over more time - good anti-noise for a noisy panel, with barely any added lag.
  7. Baseline tracking: the resting reference, the « zero » each electrode is measured against (delta = baseline - filtered). It drifts slowly with temperature, humidity and dust, so it is re-centred continuously; re-centring freezes while a zone is held, so a long press is never quietly swallowed. Set idle level (rebase) retakes it at once.
    The Tuning tab picks who maintains it: Chip (MPR121), recommended (well tuned, free), or Firmware, one step every rate frames, only useful to control that speed yourself. Example: filtered reads 902, 901, 898 while the memorised baseline stays 900: the delta stays ~0 at rest, and only a real touch moves it.

Timing

The control loop runs at 1 kHz. The three sensors are read one per frame (round-robin), so each refreshes about every 3 ms and the decision adds only a few milliseconds - below what is perceptible.

The real limit is the MPR121's own sampling rate, not how fast the firmware reads it.

Notes

Averaging and Debounce are independent - one filters the value, the other filters the duration. Debounce does not need to be ≥ avg.

Averaging dilutes a spike's height but stretches it over avg frames; debounce rejects anything short, whatever its height. A one-frame spike of 150 averaged over 3 becomes ~50 for 3 frames in a row - enough to sneak past a debounce of 3 that would have dropped it at avg 1.

That is why the wizard keeps avg low and leans on debounce as the main anti-spike lever.

Not to be confused with the old detection.

The Chip / Firmware choice only decides who maintains the resting reference.

The touch decision itself - threshold, release margin, debounce - is always made by the firmware, in both modes.

See Old vs new.

Old vs new detection

The stock mai_pico firmware and this one decide a touch in fundamentally different places. This is not a setting - the new way is now the only way; there is no switch back.

Two separate questions - do not mix them.
(1) Who decides a touch? The firmware. Always. That is what « new (software) » means, and it is not a choice.
(2) Who keeps the baseline number fresh? A bookkeeping detail, set in Tuning (Chip / Firmware). The baseline is just an input to the decision; whoever maintains it, the decision itself stays in the firmware.

So « new detection with Chip baseline » is not « going back to hardware » - it is the new detection, with the chip doing a clerk's job it happens to be good at.

MPR121 chip (front-end)          firmware (the referee)
-----------------------          -------------------------------
measures the signal        --->  delta = baseline - filtered
keeps the baseline fresh*  --->  compares to YOUR threshold
(gain, filter)                   hysteresis, debounce  ->  press!

* or the firmware keeps it itself: Tuning > Resting reference

Native (stock) detection

Each MPR121 decided on its own, inside the chip; the firmware only read a touched / not-touched flag.

Sensitivity was a coarse offset (about -30 to +30) around fixed base thresholds (touch 22, release 15), nudged one step at a time with sense + / sense -.

You could never see by how much a zone had passed - only that it had.

New (software) detection

The firmware reads each electrode's filtered value and resting reference and decides for itself: delta = baseline - filtered against an absolute per-zone threshold, with a release margin, a debounce count and optional averaging.

The MPR121 is still very much used - as a clean front-end (gain, filter) and as the keeper of the resting reference.

That visibility is what makes everything else possible: the traces, the logs, the auto-calibration and the live calibration all need to know how far a zone moved. None of them can exist on top of a bare on/off flag.

Native - pros and cons

  • + Simplest, lowest latency: the chip reports a press directly.
  • + Negligible I2C traffic; uses the MPR121's optimised detection.
  • - Coarse: an offset around a fixed base, no absolute per-zone value.
  • - No fine hysteresis or per-zone debounce, and no view of the noise.
  • - Hard to remove occasional spurious triggers precisely.

New - pros and cons

  • + Absolute per-zone threshold, hysteresis and debounce - set each zone just above its own noise.
  • + Live delta / baseline per zone (and the trace), so tuning is visible, not guesswork.
  • + Directly attacks the "too sensitive / false triggers" problem; the front-end (gain, filter) stays tunable.
  • - More moving parts and a little more code.
  • - Reads every electrode and decides in firmware - a few ms more latency and more bus traffic than a bare hardware flag.

Performance - is the new path fast enough?

Yes, comfortably. The loop stays at 1 kHz; the sensors are read round-robin (each about 333 Hz) and the software decision adds only a few milliseconds - imperceptible in play. Reading faster gains nothing: the MPR121's own sample rate is the real limit.

Pushing latency lower would mean flooding the shared I2C bus (it also carries the NFC reader) or shortening the chip's filters, which raises noise - the opposite of the goal.

The one clean lever, if ever needed, is DMA I2C reads; today that would be premature optimisation.

In short: the native path is the leanest and lowest-latency but blunt; the new path trades a little latency and I2C traffic for fine, visible, per-zone control - the right call when the goal is to stop unwanted triggers without dulling real touches.

Avoiding false triggers

A zone that triggers untouched almost always has its threshold set below its own resting noise. The fix is to read the noise, then set the threshold just above it.

  1. Open Live and leave the glass untouched. Watch the delta of each zone: that is the resting noise floor.
  2. Note the highest resting delta a zone reaches on its own.
  3. In Tuning, set that zone's threshold a little above that figure - enough margin that noise never reaches it, low enough that a real touch clears it easily.
  4. If a zone still scintillates at the limit, raise hyst (release margin) and/or debounce.
  5. If the resting delta has drifted away from zero, press Set idle level to retake the baseline untouched.
  6. When it behaves, press Save to flash - nothing survives a power cycle until you do.

Doing this by hand for 34 zones is tedious - Auto Calibration measures both numbers for you.

Auto Calibration

In one line: the wizard listens to your panel and works out every threshold for you, instead of you guessing 34 numbers.

It needs two facts per zone: how much the zone wiggles on its own (noise), and how far it moves when you press it (press peak). A good threshold sits comfortably between the two.

press peak  ~120  ####################   <-- a real press reaches here
                                       |
threshold     40  ------------------   <-- put the bar in the gap
                                       |
noise ceiling  8  ##                   <-- the zone never gets past here alone

The five steps

  1. Noise & auto-tune - about a minute, hands off the glass. The wizard first resets the board itself (no need to press Set idle level), reads the native noise, then tries a couple of reactivity-first tweaks - a little averaging, a little debounce - and keeps one only if it cuts the worst zone's noise by a clear margin. An action log shows every step (« edit param avg 1 → 2 », « keeping avg 1 ») with a live all-zones trace underneath. A final 15 s pass fixes each zone's noise floor.
  2. Press - zone by zone, at your own pace: press firmly three times, then tap briefly five times (about once a second) so the wizard learns both a firm press and a fast in-game tap. It counts real presses, not a clock. A short beep marks each press and tap, a double beep finishes a zone, and the next zone's name is spoken; toggle Sound off if you prefer quiet. A live trace of the targeted zone (with its threshold and measured noise) sits under the buttons. If a different pad answers strongest, a check mapping chip offers a one-click remap. Jump to any zone from the strip to redo it.
  3. Results - the suggested threshold per zone, placed midway between its noise and its fast taps. The midpoint matters because a hovering finger between two rapid taps keeps a residual delta above the noise: a threshold sitting right on the noise would keep its release under that residual and the zone would never let go during a spam. A zone marked risky has noise almost as strong as its press, or a threshold uncomfortably close to its fast taps: watch that one. The Quick column shows the measured fast-tap level. Skipped zones keep their current value. The recommended global settings are seeded from step 1's auto-tune and stay editable here.
  4. Verify - the suggested thresholds go to the board, then a full minute hands-off counts every real trigger a zone fires on its own. Apply & re-verify applies the ticked raises and runs another minute automatically, looping until a clean pass with no false trigger. You can also raise or lower zones by hand and apply once.
  5. Profile - stores the whole thing (including the measured noise, press and fast-tap numbers) as a profile you can re-apply later.

Applying sends the values to the board live.

They are only permanent after Save to flash.

To watch for phantom triggers any time, the Last triggers panel on the Live tab lists real firmware triggers as they happen.

Live Calibration

Not recommended - we keep this feature for fun. In one line: it watches you actually play and finds the zones whose threshold is set too high.

On this cabinet, prefer Auto Calibration.

Live calibration only lowers thresholds, and Mai Pico's standby noise can be mistaken for a missed press - so it lowers a zone that was fine and makes false triggers worse.

Use it only on a very quiet panel, or as a second opinion after Auto Calibration.

Auto Calibration measures a firm, deliberate press. In a real song you tap fast and light, so a zone can reach 80 % of its calibrated peak and be missed. Those are near-misses: a clear gesture that never triggered.

press A    72  ####################     -> hit (counted)
threshold  50  ------------------------
press B    46  #############            -> NEAR-MISS: you meant it, nothing happened
noise       7  #                        -> ignored, too small and too brief

How it tells a real gesture from noise

For each zone it follows the resting noise level, then watches every « bump » that rises clearly above it and lasts more than a frame or two.

Noise is small and brief; a finger is big and lasts.

Slides work naturally too - each zone follows its own bump, overlaps included.

Each bump is then classified against the firmware's own decision:
hit (it triggered),
tight (it triggered, but only just),
near-miss (clear gesture, no trigger).

Using it

  1. Start, then play a song normally. Pause and resume freely; the table updates a couple of times a second.
  2. Stop & review - zones with repeated near-misses get a suggested lower threshold, placed just under the strongest missed gesture (never below the noise).
  3. Accept them one by one or all at once, then Save to flash.

Auto-apply (off by default) lowers a zone live, in the middle of play, once it has missed repeatedly and gone quiet for ~10 s. Handy, but you are tuning blind - leave it off until you trust the numbers.

A tap that passed the threshold and still did not trigger was blocked by debounce, not by the threshold.

It is shown as a near-miss for information, but lowering the threshold would not help - lower debounce on instead.

Logs

In one line: live counters that never stop, plus a sample recorder you start by hand when you want a deep trace.

The per-zone stats run continuously while the board is connected. They keep three numbers:

  • Peak - the strongest delta ever seen. Your presses live here.
  • Noise - the highest delta reached while the zone was not active. This is the number a threshold must clear.
  • Triggers - how many times the zone fired. Left alone on a quiet panel, this should stay at 0; anything else is a phantom trigger.

The sample recording, by contrast, only runs after you press Start recording in the Logs tab. Samples are kept in a rolling window (the oldest are dropped), and can be exported as CSV or JSON to study offline - that is how the noisy zones of this cabinet were first mapped.

Typical use: leave the cabinet alone for a few minutes, come back, and read the Triggers column. Any zone above zero needs a higher threshold (or more debounce).

Auto-save to disk

The Logs tab can also write every recording straight to your disk, so you never have to remember an export.

Pick a folder once with Choose folder. In the Chrome prompt, tick Allow on every visit: the page then keeps the folder across reloads and starts saving on its own, with no further click.

Each visit writes one file, named for the moment it started. Files are filed by date: <folder>/YYYY/MM/mpico-session-...csv, one folder per year, then per month. The file grows as the session runs: new samples are appended about once a minute, and once more when the board is unplugged or the page is closed.

If the browser forgets the permission (it may ask again after a while), the Logs tab shows a paused state: click Choose folder once to allow it again. Disable stops auto-save and forgets the folder.

Profiles & saving

Two very different « saves »

Every change you make is applied to the board immediately, but lives only in its memory. Two separate things decide what survives:

  • Save to flash - writes the current settings into the board itself, so it boots on them. The orange banner is there to remind you something is not saved yet.
  • Profiles - named snapshots kept in this browser, so you can juggle several setups (a cautious one, an aggressive one, one per panel) and switch back.

Applying a profile sends it to the board but does not make it permanent - you still need Save to flash.

Because nothing is written until you ask, the fastest way to undo a bad experiment is simply to unplug the board: it comes back on its last saved settings.

Profiles are also how Auto Calibration hands you its result: it builds one rather than overwriting your setup behind your back.

Connection

This page talks to the board over WebSerial, so it needs Chrome or Edge. Press Connect and pick the board in the browser prompt.

The board exposes several USB interfaces. Choose the one labelled Command Line (the .2 interface) - not the game input interface. Its USB id is VID 0ca3 / PID 0021.

Once connected, the command prompt is mpico>. The page sends feed on automatically, which starts the live frames and makes the board republish its configuration. Use Disconnect to hand the port back cleanly before opening it in another program.

Protocol

The board emits two kinds of machine line, plus plain console text.

Live frame - F

One per frame, 74 space-separated tokens:

F  <36 filtered>  <36 delta>  <16-hex zone bitmap>

36 filtered readings then 36 delta values, one per electrode (0–35). The final token is a 64-bit bitmap of the active zones, as 16 hex characters.

Configuration - C

Emitted on feed on and after every setting change: C {json}, carrying the per-zone thresholds, the mapping, and every processing parameter (hyst, avg, filter, gain, debounce, baseline mode, rate) plus the saved flag.
